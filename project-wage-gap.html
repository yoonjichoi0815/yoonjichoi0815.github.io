Wage Gap Analysis: Minimum vs. Living Wage

Term: April, 2025 - May, 2025 (AMS 325 Group Project)

Team Members: Rix Chisholm, Yoonji Choi (Myself), Gyujeong Park

Tools & Libraries: Python (pandas, matplotlib, seaborn, scikit-learn, scipy), Jupyter Notebook, Google Colab

🎯 Objective

To analyze the discrepancy between minimum wage and living wage across 50 U.S. states, and explore how this gap relates to the cost of living and welfare participation. We aimed to discover hidden patterns of economic vulnerability using clustering and statistical analysis.

❓ Key Research Questions

1. How large is the wage gap in each state?

2. Which states are most economically burdened by that gap?

3. Is there a correlation between wage gap, cost of living, and SNAP participation?

4. How can we categorize states by economic vulnerability using clustering?

📈 Analysis Process

🔹 Data Preprocessing

  • Standardized datasets from multiple sources and merged them using pandas
  • Handled missing values and ensured consistent formatting for all states
  • Calculated key indicators:
    • Wage Gap = Living Wage – Minimum Wage
    • Living Wage Affordability Index (LWAI) = Minimum Wage / Living Wage
    • SNAP Participation Rate based on population and households

🔹 Exploratory Data Analysis (EDA)

  • Visualized wage gaps and affordability index using matplotlib and seaborn
  • Ranked states by affordability and highlighted top/bottom performers
  • Created threshold brackets (50%, 60%, 70%, 80%) for LWAI and mapped how many states fall below each level
  • Performed Linear Regression between Cost of Living Index (COLI) and Living Wage
  • Conducted T-Tests to assess whether SNAP participation differs between high vs. low COLI states
    • Result: No statistically significant difference (p > 0.05)

🔹 Clustering Analysis

  • Used K-Means Clustering to categorize states based on:
    • Cost of Living Index (COLI)
    • Living Wage
    • SNAP Participation Rate
  • Applied the Elbow Method to determine the optimal number of clusters
    • Found that K = 4 minimized within-cluster inertia before flattening
  • Interpreted each cluster:
    • Cluster 0: High COLI & wage, moderate SNAP – relatively stable
    • Cluster 1: Low COLI & wage, high SNAP – high welfare dependency
    • Cluster 2: High COLI, wage, SNAP – economically high-risk
    • Cluster 3: All indicators low – potentially underserved in welfare access

📊 Key Findings

🔸 Wage Inadequacy

  • None of the 50 states had a Living Wage Affordability Index (LWAI) of 1.0
  • 86% of states had LWAI < 0.70
  • States like Hawaii, New York, and New Hampshire showed the largest wage gaps

🔸 SNAP & Policy Insight

  • States like Alaska and Vermont provide >$300/month in SNAP, others < $150
  • SNAP participation varies from 7% to 20%+ (e.g., New Mexico highest)
  • No significant statistical relationship between COLI and SNAP participation (p > 0.05)
  • → Highlights the importance of policy accessibility and design

🔸 Clustering (K = 4)

  • Cluster 0: High COLI & living wage, moderate SNAP – relatively stable
  • Cluster 1: Low COLI & wage, high SNAP – high welfare dependency
  • Cluster 2: High COLI, wage, and SNAP – high economic risk
  • Cluster 3: All indicators low – possibly underserved in welfare access

💡 Insight

  • Even in high-COLI states, SNAP participation was not always higher, indicating that institutional design and access play a larger role than just economic conditions.
  • A systemic issue exists across the U.S., where minimum wages are insufficient to meet basic living costs in most states. Structural policy reform is needed.
